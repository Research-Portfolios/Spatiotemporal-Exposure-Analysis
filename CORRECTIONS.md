# Corrections (4 October 2026)

The README, notebook, figures and tables in this repository show the two manuscript drafts as they stood on 18 September 2026. The drafts have since been revised, and the statements listed below are corrected as follows. The README, notebook, figures and tables have not been changed.

**Census blocks.** The README (Abstracts, "2010.") and the notebook (the 2010 abstract; both Plain Language Summaries; and section 1, Analysis Environment and Data Summary, in its code comment and in the data summary table from results/frame_summary.csv) describe the 6,234,988 census blocks, or 6.2 million, as all the census blocks of the contiguous United States and the District of Columbia. They are the census blocks that held every 2010 resident of that area, not all of its census blocks.

**2010 summary.** The notebook's Plain Language Summary of the 2010 cross-section says that people of different racial and ethnic groups "do not breathe the same air" and speaks of what "each group breathed at home in 2010". The current draft says that they "do not live with the same outdoor air": the values are modeled outdoor concentrations at home, not the air each person breathes. The summary says that most of the nitrogen dioxide differences "come from conditions within a few kilometers of home"; the current draft says that most of the nitrogen dioxide excess comes "from local rather than regional differences in pollution". The summary also says that neighborhoods graded as risky in the 1930s "still have more nitrogen dioxide today"; the values are modeled for 2010, and the current draft says that those neighborhoods "still had more nitrogen dioxide in 2010".

**Nitrogen dioxide ratio.** The notebook's Plain Language Summary of change over 2000-2015 says that the ratio between the groups became larger because concentrations fell in roughly constant proportion. A fall in constant proportion would leave the ratio unchanged. In the current draft the ratio grew because modeled nitrogen dioxide fell by a larger share where non-Hispanic White residents live than where Black, Hispanic and Asian residents live. A separately built model on a 1-kilometer grid did not reproduce this growth for Black residents.

**Benefit incidence.** The notebook (Paper 2, Section 4.7, Benefit incidence; Figure P2-10, figures/fig02_benefit_incidence.png; Table P2-8, results/table7_economics_benefit_incidence.csv) reports that every minority group's benefit share exceeded its population share for both pollutants, from a calculation that applied one national death rate to every group. The current draft applies each group's own national death rate at ages 30 and over, from the Underlying Cause of Death, 1999-2020 database of the National Center for Health Statistics on CDC WONDER, queried on 24 September 2026; section 6 of the notebook lists group-specific baseline mortality as further work. On that basis the non-Hispanic White share of the avoided deaths exceeded its population share for both pollutants, the Hispanic and non-Hispanic Asian shares fell below their population shares, and the non-Hispanic Black share was about equal to its population share. These shares rest on a response function common to all groups, so they are not a bound in either direction. With one rate for every group, the ratios describe the distribution of the concentration decline, not of the modeled avoided deaths. The totals, ratios and monetized values of that section, and the statement in section 6 of the notebook that higher starting concentrations yield more avoided deaths per person, are superseded; the revised values will appear with the paper.

**Ozone designation.** The README (Abstracts, "2000-2015.") and the notebook (the 2000-2015 abstract) state that eight-hour ozone nonattainment designation in 2004 was followed by NO<sub>2</sub> reductions averaging about 1.3 to 1.8 ppb, whose size depends on how designated counties were selected. In the current draft, about 1.3 to 1.8 ppb is the event-study estimate across the two codings of designated counties; across the identifying assumptions, the reductions relative to never-designated counties ranged from about zero to 1.8 ppb. Whether designation reduced NO<sub>2</sub>, and by how much, depends on whether designated counties differed from never-designated counties in levels, in trends, or in the shocks that preceded designation, and the data do not distinguish these explanations.

**Wildfire smoke.** The caption of Figure P2-15 in the notebook (Paper 2, Section 4.10, Wildfire smoke exposure) says that all groups other than non-Hispanic White are pooled because smoke exposure is only resolvable that way at county level. That reason does not hold: the current draft weights the same county predictions by each group's own 2010 county population and reports a ratio for each group, which will appear with the paper.

**Credits.** The README (Data), docs/data_sources.md and the notebook (section 7, Data Availability) omit the attribution sentence that the current drafts carry, and the notebook's "Unrestricted." paragraph attributes the concentration surfaces to Wang et al. (2020); none of the three gives a licence for their deposit. The concentration surfaces are the decomposition by Wang et al. (2020) of the block predictions of the CACES v1 empirical models of Kim et al. (2020), deposited as Wang (2022), https://doi.org/10.17632/bz8pdbcvf2.3, under CC BY 4.0. This repository includes concentration estimates developed by the Center for Air, Climate and Energy Solutions (CACES) using spatially decomposed v1 empirical models as described in Wang et al. (2020).

## Addendum (8 October 2026)

**Titles.** The README and the notebook give the titles of the two manuscripts as they stood on 18 September 2026. The manuscript on the 2010 cross-section, there titled "Air pollution exposure disparities by race, ethnicity and historical redlining grade across 6.2 million United States census blocks", is now titled "Racial and ethnic disparities in modeled air pollution across distance scales and historical redlining grades". The manuscript on change over 2000-2015, there titled "Changes in absolute and relative racial and ethnic disparities in air pollution exposure across United States census blocks, 2000-2015", is now titled "Trends in racial and ethnic disparities in modeled air pollution on absolute and relative measures".

**Ozone designation and the NO<sub>x</sub> Budget Trading Program.** The notebook (Paper 2, Section 4.6, Ozone nonattainment designation; and section 6, Discussion and Limitations) says that the overlap between the states of the concurrent NO<sub>x</sub> Budget Trading Program and the counties designated for ozone nonattainment was not measured. The current draft measured it: under the strict coding, 299 of the 384 designated counties lie in the program's states. Within those states the NO<sub>2</sub> reduction associated with designation remained; outside them it was smaller and depended on the coding and on population weighting. The association was therefore concentrated in the program's eastern and midwestern states, and the design cannot separate designation from changes, under the program or otherwise, that differed between designated and never-designated counties there.

**README.** The first paragraph of this notice says that the README has not been changed. On 6 October 2026 its plain links to the nbviewer and Google Colab views of the notebook were replaced by badges that link to the same views. Nothing else in the README has changed, and the notebook, figures and tables have not been changed.

**Data credits and requirements.** In addition to the Credits item above, which gives the attribution sentence and the licence of the four-scale deposit, the Data table of the README and docs/data_sources.md give the following credits only in part. The concentrations come from the CACES v1 empirical models (Kim et al., 2020); the CACES data are free and publicly available for non-commercial purposes and may not be used for commercial purposes without prior written permission. The census block populations are from IPUMS NHGIS Version 19.0, doi:10.18128/D050.V19.0 (Manson et al., 2024). The primary jobs by home block and work block are from the U.S. Census Bureau's LEHD Origin-Destination Employment Statistics (LODES), version 7.5, https://lehd.ces.census.gov/data/lodes, accessed on 3 and 17 September 2026. The daily wildfire smoke predictions are version 1 (V1) of the Harvard Dataverse deposit doi:10.7910/DVN/DJVMTV (Childs et al., 2024), and the article that describes them is Childs et al. (2022), Environmental Science & Technology 56(19): 13607-13621. The README (Reproducing) names pandas and Pillow as the requirements of the notebook; its tables also need Jinja2.
